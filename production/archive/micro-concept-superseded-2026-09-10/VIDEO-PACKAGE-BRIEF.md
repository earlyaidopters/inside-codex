# Inside Codex video package

Stage: spark
Updated: September 10, 2026. This is a proposed video direction, not an approved Concept Lock or a full filming script.

## Premise and viewer

Mark used Astra and Blender to build a browser-playable world for his community to practise Codex workflows. The video teaches the viewer how to plan a useful learning experience, build its world, and test it before sharing it.

Primary viewer: an AI builder, educator, community owner, or operator who wants to turn something they teach into an interactive experience. Familiar category anchor: building with GPT-6 Astra. Codex education is the demonstrated use case. Other subjects, employee onboarding, and classroom applications are proposed adaptations, not tested outcomes.

Demand class: current-catalyst opportunity with proven channel adjacency. The recent Astra game wave supplies the catalyst; Mark's recent Astra videos supply channel adjacency. Educational demand is a promising hypothesis supported by specific X examples, not a measured market conclusion. Do not describe the project as the first educational Astra game.

## Evidence and truth boundaries

Research lock: September 10, 2026.

1. Owned working artifact: https://wintry-soul-6awh.here.now/ . Twelve missions, a separate capstone, exported learning resources, and a World Tour. Deterministic teaching simulations; the webpage does not run visitors' actual Codex agents or connect their accounts.
2. Owned build history: `research/THREAD-REVIEW-2026-09-10.md` and its three detailed reports. Original plan, implementation problems, fixes, testing, hosted verification, and requested post-release refinements.
3. X educational adjacency: Georgia Witchel's chip-learning game and Michael Puscar's reported educational games. See `research/X-ASTRA-GAMES-2026-09-10.md` for original links and limits.
4. X tension: Anshu's own assessment that his game got stale after the first few minutes; Sunwake discussion distinguishing an experience from a finished game. These support a question about depth, not a claim that Astra games universally waste time.
5. Owned channel retention: the recent effort-level video's opening held attention; extended setup delayed useful comparisons. Apply by showing a real interaction early and limiting prompt/plan exposition.

No human learning study has established that this teaches faster, improves retention, or creates Codex mastery. Avoid “learn every corner of Codex.” Software checks establish specific behavior, not educational effectiveness.

Main production through 0.1.0: approximately 17 hours 24 minutes and 8,309,291 tracked tokens. Later World Tour and answer/skip patches are excluded. These are tracked tokens, not generated output tokens or a dollar bill. Do not promise a cheap, instant, one-prompt reproduction.

## Promise contract

- Pain: impressive AI demos are easier to recognize than a useful experience someone can actually navigate and complete.
- Outcome: understand a repeatable process for planning, building, checking, and sharing a playable lesson.
- Prerequisite: access to an AI coding environment and willingness to iterate; reproducing this exact production requires the documented tools and substantial compute. Playing the existing game requires no API key.
- Three promises: plan the learning and define completion; build a coherent 3D world with Astra and Blender; test, improve, and publish the experience.
- First payoff: within 30 seconds, show a player action and its visible consequence in a real mission, after a brief World Tour opening.
- Satisfaction: viewer sees the actual plan, a concrete asset-to-browser build path, a failure repaired through testing, and the hosted result with a practical starting point for their own subject.
- Resource: Mark wants to show the community resource entry and access path. Verify the actual Skool entry and its contents before filming or naming its attachments. Do not imply an uploaded package is already available without checking.

## Minimal story spine

Provisional runtime: 10–12 minutes, an editorial target rather than a measured optimum.

| Section job | Visible proof | Viewer application | New value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Show why the world is worth entering | Tour excerpt plus one mission's action and consequence | Choose a lesson with an observable decision | Immediate proof of the experience |
| Establish who it serves | Community resource access path, once verified; one relevant X educational example | Pick a real audience and a specific problem | A reason to build beyond visual novelty |
| Explain the plan | Original production plan paired with resulting mission, guide, and completion criteria | Define learner action, feedback, takeaway, and acceptance checks | A usable planning method |
| Explain the build | Actual Blender mascot and an authored asset in the browser; original build records | Give the agent a consistent visual direction and concrete deliverables | How the tools fit together |
| Explain what made it ready | A gameplay/interaction failure and repair; a visual/camera failure and repair; concise testing receipt | Test the player's complete route as well as individual functions | Why an attractive first build needs iteration |
| Show delivery and adaptation | Hosted experience, real toolkit contents, later Tour and answer/skip changes | Publish, observe friction, refine; start with one lesson in another subject | A concrete next step and an honest scope model |

Keep cost and elapsed time in the testing/iteration section, with the accounting boundary visible. Do not front-load environment setup, tool inventories, or the entire chronological log. If detailed performance work is included, use one comparable before/after and explain its effect on the player.

The original plan is evidence of intent, not proof that every step was followed. In particular, the scaffold expanded before the polished sample and later work repaired shallow interactions. Show where execution diverged and what the next plan should improve.

## Reveal contract

Show the finished experience immediately. Reserve the extent of the iteration, specific breakages, and production effort for the build/testing sections. Promise the process openly; do not conceal prerequisites or imply the game was effortless. The strongest later payoff is seeing why a first version became something Mark was comfortable giving to his community.
